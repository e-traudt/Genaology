# Forensic Genealogy

*Last revised: 2026-11-01*

Forensic genealogy covers two overlapping practices: **investigative genetic genealogy (IGG / FGG)**, the use of consumer-style SNP profiles and genealogical research to identify suspects and unidentified human remains, and **traditional forensic genealogy**, the use of records research to identify heirs, next of kin and military dead. This page tracks both, with emphasis on IGG because that is where the science, the industry and the law are moving.

## Current consensus

- **IGG is now routine.** Since the Golden State Killer arrest in April 2018, IGG has produced well over a thousand identifications in the United States and is spreading to Canada, Australia, the UK and Sweden. Most weeks bring several new resolutions. **[A]**
- **The lab stack has shifted to whole-genome sequencing (WGS).** Array genotyping on degraded or low-quantity remains often fails. Othram's "Forensic-Grade Genome Sequencing", Bode Technology's WGS workflow (approved by New York's Wadsworth Center), and NIJ-funded validation studies have made WGS the default for difficult samples. Othram claims it is the only lab with its capability; treat that as marketing. **[B]**
- **Only two consumer databases permit law-enforcement matching.** GEDmatch (via GEDmatch PRO) and FamilyTreeDNA allow it, both opt-out by default for new users in most jurisdictions; AncestryDNA, 23andMe and MyHeritage refuse. Database size for IGG is therefore roughly 1.5–2 million opted-in kits, far below the consumer majors. **[B]**
- **Regulation is state-led.** Maryland (2021) and Montana (2021) passed the first statutes. Maryland requires judicial authorisation, lab accreditation, and limits IGG to murder, rape, felony sexual assault and public-safety threats. Montana requires a warrant. Utah has since enacted a statute. The US Department of Justice interim policy (2019) governs federal agencies but is not binding. Maryland's 2026 session carried SB0678, amending the affidavit requirements for IGG authorisation, with a stated effective date of 1 October 2026; final status to be confirmed. **[A for statutes, B for 2026 bill status]**
- **Academic IGG centres are a growing part of the ecosystem.** Ramapo College's IGG Center (New Jersey) takes referred cases and trains students; Othram's DNASolves and the DNA Doe Project crowdsource funding and volunteer research. **[B]**
- **Courts have mostly not yet ruled on the core Fourth Amendment questions.** Admissibility challenges have generally failed because IGG produces a lead, not evidence; the confirmatory STR match is what goes to trial. Watch for a test case on warrantless database searching. **[B]**

## Themes to track

1. **WGS versus array for forensic samples.** Cost, turnaround, and whether public-sector labs (state crime labs, the FBI) adopt in-house WGS rather than outsourcing to Othram or Bode.
2. **Database access and consent.** Any policy change at GEDmatch (owned by Qiagen since 2024) or FamilyTreeDNA; any move by Ancestry or 23andMe/TTAM; any subpoena or warrant served on a non-participating database.
3. **State legislation.** New statutes modelled on Maryland; amendments like SB0678; bills in states with active IGG programmes (Texas, Florida, California, Washington).
4. **Case law.** First appellate ruling squarely on warrantless IGG; any suppression motion that succeeds.
5. **Standards and accreditation.** SWGDAM IGG guidelines, ISO 17025 scope for genealogy, the proposed federal accreditation requirement, and professional credentials (e.g. the IGG Accreditation Board).
6. **International spread.** UK Home Office pilots, Swedish prosecutions, Canadian provincial policies, EU GDPR constraints.
7. **Overlap with the other tracks.** The same WGS and haplotree tools used on degraded forensic samples are the ones used on ancient and dynastic burials (see [Genghis-Khan.md](Genghis-Khan.md) and [DNA-Testing.md](DNA-Testing.md)). Methods validated in forensics tend to appear in archaeogenetics within a year or two.

## Practical guidance (stable)

- If you want your kit to help solve cases, upload to GEDmatch and opt in to law-enforcement matching, and set FamilyTreeDNA to opt in. If you do not, check both settings, since defaults have changed several times.
- Volunteer routes: DNA Doe Project, Trans Doe Task Force, Ramapo IGG Center programmes, Othram's DNASolves funding pages.
- Traditional forensic genealogy (heir search, next-of-kin for unclaimed persons, military repatriation via DPAA) remains document-driven; the Board for Certification of Genealogists and the Council for the Advancement of Forensic Genealogy set professional standards.

## History of the question

- 2018-04: Golden State Killer identified via GEDmatch. IGG goes public.
- 2019-05: GEDmatch switches to opt-in after the Utah assault case; database for LE shrinks sharply. DOJ interim policy issued (effective Nov 2019).
- 2019-12: GEDmatch acquired by Verogen; 2024: acquired by Qiagen.
- 2021: Maryland and Montana statutes. Maryland's takes effect October 2021.
- 2022–2024: Othram scales WGS; DNA Doe Project passes hundreds of IDs; Ramapo IGG Center founded (2022).
- 2025: 23andMe bankruptcy raises the question of law-enforcement access to its data under new ownership; TTAM commits to existing policy.
- 2026: Maryland SB0678; continued weekly case resolutions; WGS now standard for degraded remains.

## Open questions

- Will a court find that an opted-in consumer database search still requires a warrant?
- Will any of the big three consumer databases ever open to law enforcement, voluntarily or by compulsion?
- Will federal legislation harmonise the state patchwork?
- Can public labs bring WGS in-house at scale, or does IGG remain dependent on two or three private vendors?
